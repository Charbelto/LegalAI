\title{Specialised but Structured: A Controlled Comparison of Coordination
Topologies for PEFT-Tuned Multi-Agent Compliance Question Answering}

\author{Charbel~Toumieh~and~Omid~Chatrabgoun%
\thanks{C. Toumieh is with WMG, University of Warwick, Coventry, UK.}%
\thanks{O. Chatrabgoun is with Coventry University, Coventry, UK.}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Work on multi-agent language-model systems divides into two questions that are
rarely asked together: \emph{how many} agents to use, and \emph{how to wire}
the ones you have. The first has recently been answered largely in the negative
--- a single well-configured agent matches or beats multi-agent coordination on
general reasoning --- but those comparisons give every agent the same
unmodified model, so their ``specialists'' are one generalist behind several
prompts. This paper asks the second question under the condition the first
assumes away: once each expert is a genuinely different, separately fine-tuned
model, which coordination topology combines them best? We build a
retrieval-grounded EU AI Act compliance system in which three domain experts run
as three distinct open-weight models on one 8\,GB consumer GPU --- Llama 3.2 3B
(legal), Qwen2.5 3B (news) and Granite 3.1 2B (general) --- each 4-bit quantised
and specialised by QLoRA on its own domain corpus. Holding the experts,
retrieval corpus, query set and reference answers fixed, we vary only
coordination structure across three patterns that are structurally distinct
rather than merely different: a strict sequential chain, full concurrency, and a
converging dependency graph. Because a topology comparison among specialised
agents cannot by itself show that specialisation helped, we run every topology
twice --- once with the LoRA adapters loaded and once with the identical base
models untuned --- giving 30 queries $\times$ 3 topologies $\times$ 3 repeats
$\times$ 2 arms (540 runs) scored on lexical alignment, LLM-judge quality,
abstention behaviour and node-level operational telemetry, with paired
non-parametric tests over queries. We find that no topology significantly
outperformed another on judged quality (adapted arm: \textsc{all} $1.80$,
\textsc{dag} $1.77$, \textsc{parallel} $1.73$; all pairwise Holm-corrected
$p = 0.92$, $|\delta| \leq 0.07$), that PEFT specialisation
left judged quality statistically indistinguishable from the untuned control
($p = 1.00$ on all three topologies, $|\delta| \leq 0.08$) while reducing the
expert abstention rate from $22.3\%$ to $1.6\%$, and
that this behavioural change was bought at close to double the end-to-end
latency ($244$--$249$\,s against $124$--$129$\,s; $p \leq 8.3\times10^{-7}$,
$\delta \geq 0.92$) and significantly higher token cost ($p \leq 0.004$),
with no significant latency difference among topologies within either arm.
We further report that on a
single GPU nominal concurrency buys almost nothing: three co-resident experts
generating simultaneously achieved a measured speedup of only
$1.07\times$ over sequential execution, so ``parallel'' topologies pay
coordination cost for a structural property the hardware does not deliver.
Because the judge (DeepSeek V4 Flash) now generates none of the answers it
scores, the self-preference-bias caveat that constrained our earlier
single-versus-multi study no longer applies.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%=============================================================================

An \emph{AI agent}, in the sense used throughout this paper, is a language model
placed in a control loop: it is given a goal, some memory of what has happened so
far, and a set of actions it can take, and it decides for itself which action to
take next rather than emitting a single forward pass. The actions available here
are retrieval calls against a document corpus, delegation to a specialised
sub-agent, and generation of text.

A \emph{multi-agent system} composes several such agents, usually specialised by
role, and coordinates them through an orchestration layer so that the output of
one becomes the input or the trigger of another. The orchestration layer is not
incidental: it determines which agents run, in what order, on what state, and how
their outputs are combined. We refer to this wiring as the system's
\emph{topology}, and it is the independent variable of this study.

The intuition behind multi-agent design is that decomposing a hard problem into
specialised sub-problems mirrors how human teams work. The costs are equally
concrete. Every additional agent adds coordination overhead, inter-agent
communication, redundant context handling and additional model invocations, all
of which raise latency and token cost, and each hand-off is an opportunity for
information to be lost or corrupted.

\subsection{What is already known}

Two literatures bear directly on this question, and it is worth being precise
about what each has settled, because the naive version of our research question
is already answered.

First, several 2026 studies find that multi-agent systems do not reliably
outperform single agents. Jwalapuram et al.~\cite{jwalapuram2026illusion} show
that automatically generated multi-agent systems consistently underperform
chain-of-thought with self-consistency while costing up to an order of magnitude
more. Tran and Kiela~\cite{tran2026singleagent} report that under a fixed
reasoning-token budget single agents match or beat multi-agent systems on
multi-hop reasoning, and Xu et al.~\cite{xu2026oneflow} reach a similar
conclusion across seven benchmarks. Cemri et al.~\cite{cemri2025mast} give the
complementary diagnosis, taxonomising fourteen failure modes of multi-agent
systems into specification issues, inter-agent misalignment and task
verification. Earlier scaling work is consistent with this: more calls or more
agents help only under specific conditions~\cite{li2024moreagents,
chen2024morellmcalls}.

\emph{We therefore do not claim novelty for the observation that a single agent
can beat a multi-agent system.} That result is published, and in earlier work on
this same system we reproduced it: with one shared unmodified model behind every
expert, a true single-expert router matched or beat seven multi-agent topologies
on EU AI Act compliance QA while costing less.

There is, however, a shared premise in this literature that is easy to miss.
These studies hold the language model fixed and vary only the number or wiring of
agents, which means their ``specialists'' are one generalist model behind
several different prompts. Role differentiation is therefore entirely a matter of
instruction, not of parameters. If the reason coordination fails to pay is that
the agents being coordinated are substantially the same reasoner, then the
negative result is a statement about prompt-differentiated ensembles rather than
about multi-agent design as such --- and the question of how to combine
genuinely different specialists is left open rather than answered.

Second, multi-agent topology design is an established subfield rather than a gap.
GPTSwarm~\cite{zhuge2024gptswarm} treats agent systems as optimisable graphs;
MacNet~\cite{qian2024macnet} scales collaboration across topologies;
DyLAN~\cite{liu2023dylan} routes dynamically at the instance level;
AgentPrune~\cite{zhang2024agentprune} and AgentDropout~\cite{wang2025agentdropout}
sparsify communication; G-Designer~\cite{zhang2024gdesigner} and
ARG-Designer~\cite{li2025argdesigner} learn to generate the communication graph.
A 2026 survey~\cite{zhu2026masorchestrationsurvey} already taxonomises the space.

\emph{We therefore also do not claim novelty for the observation that structure
matters.}

\subsection{What is left, and what this paper does}

Four things fall between these literatures.

\textbf{Neither literature separates specialisation from structure.} The
single-versus-multi studies vary agent count with the model held fixed; the
topology studies vary wiring with the model held fixed. Neither varies what the
agents \emph{are}. Consequently no existing result distinguishes ``coordination
does not help'' from ``coordination does not help when there is nothing
substantively different to coordinate''. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning
\cite{hu2021lora, dettmers2023qlora} makes the distinction cheap to test: a
LoRA adapter per role turns prompt-differentiated agents into
parameter-differentiated ones at a few million trainable parameters each.

\textbf{Neither literature covers retrieval-grounded regulatory compliance.} The
single-versus-multi studies use reasoning benchmarks, where the model is expected
to answer. Compliance QA has the opposite failure mode: when the retrieved
statutory context does not support an answer, the correct behaviour is to abstain
and escalate. Legal systems such as ChatLaw~\cite{cui2023chatlaw} and
PAKTON~\cite{raptopoulos2025pakton} fix their agent configuration and do not ask
whether it is the right one; COMPL-AI~\cite{guldimann2024complai} benchmarks
\emph{models} against the EU AI Act rather than agent structures; legal RAG
benchmarks~\cite{guha2023legalbench, pipitone2024legalbenchrag, li2025lexrag}
evaluate retrieval and answer quality inside fixed pipelines.

\textbf{Topology work rarely reports whether nominal concurrency is real.} A
parallel topology is only parallel if the serving hardware executes its branches
concurrently. On a single accelerator hosting several models this is an empirical
question, not a property of the graph, and we measure it directly rather than
assuming it.

\textbf{Operational cost is rarely attributed to structural decisions.}
Observability tooling exposes execution traces, and topology papers report token
cost, but node-level latency attribution is seldom connected to answer quality in
one evaluation, which is what makes it possible to say \emph{which stage} of a
topology is paying for its overhead.

This paper makes the following contributions.

\begin{enumerate}
\item A controlled comparison of three structurally distinct coordination
topologies --- a strict sequential chain, full concurrency, and a converging
dependency graph --- over three \emph{parameter-differentiated} domain experts,
with the experts, retrieval corpus, query set and reference answers held fixed,
so that observed differences are attributable to coordination structure alone.
\item A factorial second arm that runs all three topologies again on the
identical base models with no adapters loaded. This is what licenses any claim
that specialisation itself mattered, and separates a specialisation effect from a
topology effect instead of confounding them.
\item A demonstration that three separately fine-tuned 2--3B experts can be
co-resident and serve a multi-agent graph on a single 8\,GB consumer GPU, with
the memory budget reported in enough detail to be reproduced or refuted
(Section~\ref{sec:memory}) --- including the finding that grouped-query
attention, not parameter count, is the binding constraint on how many experts
fit.
\item A measurement of how much nominal parallelism a single-GPU deployment
actually delivers, which bears directly on whether concurrent topologies are
worth their coordination cost outside a multi-accelerator setting.
\item A structural failure mode we name \emph{abstention propagation}, in which a
single abstaining expert silences an entire ensemble unless the aggregator is
explicitly designed against it. This biases comparisons against multi-agent
topologies for reasons unrelated to reasoning quality, and we quantify it.
\end{enumerate}

We answer three research questions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textbf{RQ1}] Given three domain-specialised (PEFT fine-tuned) expert
agents, which coordination topology --- sequential, parallel or DAG --- produces
the best judged answer quality, and at what operational cost?
\item[\textbf{RQ2}] Does PEFT specialisation itself measurably improve answer
quality over the same base models untuned, and does the ranking of topologies
survive the change?
\item[\textbf{RQ3}] How does topology affect operational cost --- latency,
execution steps, tokens --- once each expert is a distinct local model rather
than one shared model, and where in the graph is that cost incurred?
\end{enumerate}

RQ2 is deliberately not a headline claim about fine-tuning as a technique; it is
the control that makes RQ1 interpretable. Without it, a topology ranking over
specialised agents could not be distinguished from a topology ranking that would
have come out the same way with no specialisation at all.

\section{Related Work}
%=============================================================================

\subsection{Multi-agent topology and orchestration}
This is the line of work this paper contributes to. Where the
single-versus-multi literature asks \emph{how many} agents, this one asks
\emph{how they are wired}. GPTSwarm~\cite{zhuge2024gptswarm} optimises agent
graphs; MacNet~\cite{qian2024macnet} studies how collaboration scales with
topology; DyLAN~\cite{liu2023dylan} activates agents dynamically per instance;
AgentPrune~\cite{zhang2024agentprune} and AgentDropout~\cite{wang2025agentdropout}
remove redundant communication edges; G-Designer~\cite{zhang2024gdesigner} and
ARG-Designer~\cite{li2025argdesigner} generate task-conditioned communication
graphs. General frameworks~\cite{wu2023autogen, hong2023metagpt, li2023camel} and
graph execution engines~\cite{langchain2024langgraph} provide the machinery, and a
2026 survey~\cite{zhu2026masorchestrationsurvey} taxonomises the space.

Across this subfield the agents being wired together are, with few exceptions,
instances of one model differing only in prompt. Topology is optimised, learned
or pruned; the nodes themselves are interchangeable. That is a reasonable
simplification when the object of study is the graph, but it means the field's
results describe how best to route between copies of a reasoner rather than
between specialists. This paper keeps the topology comparison and changes the
nodes: three different base models, each with its own adapter trained on its own
domain data. We also depart from the subfield's usual practice of comparing
learned topologies against each other by comparing a small number of canonical,
hand-specified structures, which makes the result interpretable at the cost of
generality.

\subsection{Parameter-efficient specialisation}
LoRA~\cite{hu2021lora} freezes a pretrained model and learns low-rank updates to
its projection matrices, reducing trainable parameters by orders of magnitude
while retaining most of the quality of full fine-tuning.
QLoRA~\cite{dettmers2023qlora} adds 4-bit NF4 quantisation of the frozen base
plus paged optimisers, which is what makes several specialised models trainable
and servable on one consumer GPU --- the property this study depends on. We use
LoRA adapters not to chase state-of-the-art domain performance but as the
cheapest available way to make role differentiation a property of parameters
rather than of prompts, so that ``specialist'' means something measurable.
Multi-adapter serving is normally posed as one base model with swapped
adapters; we deliberately use three different bases, since a shared base would
reintroduce exactly the homogeneity we are trying to remove.

\subsection{Single-agent versus multi-agent evidence}
This line motivates the paper rather than being its target. The claim that
multi-agent systems are superior rests on context isolation, parallelism and
distributed decision-making. Jwalapuram
et al.~\cite{jwalapuram2026illusion} argue that the empirical support for this
comes from benchmarks that reward isolated reasoning and from weak single-agent
baselines; under fair comparison, automatic multi-agent systems underperform
chain-of-thought with self-consistency at up to ten times the cost. Tran and
Kiela~\cite{tran2026singleagent} control the reasoning-token budget and find
single agents are more information-efficient on multi-hop reasoning. Xu
et al.~\cite{xu2026oneflow} corroborate this across seven benchmarks. Cemri
et al.~\cite{cemri2025mast} annotate more than 1{,}600 execution traces across
seven frameworks and attribute failures largely to specification and inter-agent
misalignment rather than to base-model capability.

We take these results as established and as the reason for the present design.
Because all of them hold the model fixed across agents, they bound what
prompt-differentiated coordination can achieve; they do not speak to
parameter-differentiated coordination. Accordingly this paper does not include a
single-agent baseline and makes no single-versus-multi claim: with three
different models in the graph, ``one agent'' is no longer a single condition but
a choice of which of three models to keep, and the comparison would confound
agent count with model identity. The single-agent configuration remains
implemented in our system but is out of scope here.

\subsection{Legal and compliance question answering}
LegalBench~\cite{guha2023legalbench} and
LegalBench-RAG~\cite{pipitone2024legalbenchrag} evaluate legal reasoning and
retrieval; LexRAG~\cite{li2025lexrag} targets conversational legal QA;
ChatLaw~\cite{cui2023chatlaw} and PAKTON~\cite{raptopoulos2025pakton} build legal
multi-agent systems with fixed configurations;
COMPL-AI~\cite{guldimann2024complai} maps EU AI Act requirements onto model
benchmarks. None varies agent topology as an independent variable.

\subsection{Evaluation methodology}
We use LLM-as-a-judge scoring~\cite{zheng2023mtbench} alongside lexical overlap
metrics~\cite{papineni2002bleu, lin2004rouge}. Judges are known to favour their
own generations~\cite{wataoka2024selfpreference}, so we require the judge model,
the model under test and the model used to draft reference answers to be
distinct, and we report all three. Significance is assessed with paired
non-parametric tests~\cite{wilcoxon1945individual} under Holm
correction~\cite{holm1979simple}, with Cliff's delta~\cite{cliff1993dominance} as
the effect size.

\section{Methodology}
%=============================================================================

\subsection{Experimental design}
The design is a $3 \times 2$ within-subjects factorial: three coordination
topologies crossed with two specialisation arms, with every query executed in
every cell. Each cell therefore sees identical inputs, an identical retrieval
corpus, identical expert models and an identical reference answer; the only
things that vary are the routing structure and whether the LoRA adapters are
loaded. Each (query, topology, arm) cell is repeated $R$ times with a distinct
sampling seed per repeat, so that repeats capture generation variance rather than
returning identical text.

The second factor is not a secondary experiment. A topology comparison run only
over specialised agents cannot distinguish ``DAG is the best way to combine
specialists'' from ``DAG would have won anyway''. Running the identical three
topologies over the identical three base models with no adapters attached is the
minimum control that separates those two readings, and it is what RQ2 tests.

\subsection{System under test}
All topologies are execution paths through a single compiled graph of ten nodes:
planner, router, memory, retrieval, three domain experts (legal, news, general
QA), aggregator, validator and response. Holding the node implementations fixed
and varying only the edges is what isolates topology from prompt engineering.

\textbf{Three models, not one.} Unlike the topology literature and unlike our own
earlier study, the three expert nodes are backed by three \emph{different}
models, each with its own LoRA adapter (Table~\ref{tab:experts}). All three are
loaded concurrently in 4-bit NF4 quantisation and remain resident for the whole
run, so a topology that fans out to several experts is genuinely invoking several
distinct models rather than the same model several times.

\textbf{Coordination nodes.} The planner, router, memory, aggregator, validator
and response nodes also invoke a language model, but they are not domain
experts, and a fourth resident model does not fit the memory budget. They
therefore share the general-purpose expert's \emph{base} weights with the
adapter disabled. This is a deliberate design choice rather than an
implementation convenience: keeping specialisation confined to the three expert
nodes is what makes ``coordination structure over specialised experts'' the thing
being varied, instead of also silently domain-tuning the component that merges
and validates their outputs.

\textbf{Planner.} The planner is a dispatcher agent that reads the query before
any retrieval happens and emits an explicit plan: the set of experts it judges
necessary and, implicitly, the order in which their outputs should be composed.
It differs from the router in scope. The router performs a single
classification -- which domain does this query belong to -- whereas the planner
performs decomposition, and may return several experts for a query it considers
compound. In planner-based mode this plan determines the expert set; in the other
topologies the planner still runs but its plan does not control routing, which
keeps the node set constant across conditions.

\textbf{Router.} The router assigns the query to one or more domain labels.

\textbf{Retrieval.} A shared retrieval node queries the vector store and places
the retrieved chunks in state, so every expert in every topology sees the same
evidence. This is what makes the single-agent baseline retrieval-matched.

\textbf{Domain experts.} The legal expert answers from the statutory corpus and
is instructed to abstain with a fixed sentence when the retrieved context lacks
authoritative support. The news expert covers recent developments; the general QA
expert handles everything else. Which experts run, and in what order, is what
Section~\ref{sec:topologies} varies.

\textbf{Aggregator.} Merges expert outputs into a single draft. Its handling of
abstention is a design decision with experimental consequences, discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:abstention}.

\textbf{Validator and response.} The validator checks the draft against the
retrieved sources and can trigger a bounded retry; the response node performs
final formatting.

\subsection{Topologies}
\label{sec:topologies}
Three configurations are compared, chosen because they are structurally distinct
rather than merely different --- a strict chain, full concurrency, and a
converging dependency --- which spans the space of ways three peers can be
composed without multiplying conditions. They are drawn in
Figure~\ref{fig:topologies}.

\begin{figure*}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.97\textwidth]{fig0_topologies.png}
\caption{The three coordination topologies compared in this study. All are
execution paths through one graph and share a single retrieval step, so every
expert in every topology conditions on the same evidence; only the wiring between
experts differs. Five further configurations remain implemented in the system but
are out of scope and are described in Section~\ref{sec:scope} rather than drawn
here. The lower-right panel illustrates abstention propagation
(Section~\ref{sec:abstention}): under an any-abstain veto a single abstaining
expert discards its collaborators' work, whereas the all-abstain rule used here
records the abstention and preserves the answer --- the rule that produced the
$0.000$ system abstention rate reported in Section~\ref{sec:results-ablation}
despite the legal expert declining on $67\%$ of untuned runs.}
\label{fig:topologies}
\end{figure*}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{ALL (sequential).} Legal $\rightarrow$ news $\rightarrow$ general
QA, each expert conditioned on the accumulated state of its predecessors. Maximum
information flow between experts, zero concurrency, longest critical path.
\item \textbf{PARALLEL (concurrent).} All three experts run simultaneously on the
same retrieved context and are merged by the aggregator. No expert sees another's
output, so this is the minimum-coupling condition; its critical path is the
slowest single expert rather than the sum.
\item \textbf{DAG (converging).} Legal and news run concurrently and both feed
into general QA, which then feeds the aggregator. This is the intermediate case:
partial concurrency with a genuine dependency, so one expert conditions on two
others while those two remain independent of each other.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Configurations implemented but out of scope}
\label{sec:scope}
The system also implements a single-expert router (SINGLE), a conditional branch
(LEGAL-FIRST), a planner-driven dispatcher (PLANNER-BASED), a partial-parallel
pair (LEGAL-NEWS) and an expert-free verification bypass (VERIFY-ONLY). All five
remain fully functional and selectable at run time; they are excluded from this
study for scope, not because they fail. We state this explicitly because the
exclusion of SINGLE in particular is a substantive decision rather than an
oversight: with three different models in the graph, a one-agent condition is a
choice of \emph{which} model to retain, so it would confound agent count with
model identity and could not serve as a baseline. Readers interested in the
single-versus-multi question with one shared model are referred to the earlier
study on this same system.

\subsection{Abstention as a first-class outcome}
\label{sec:abstention}
In compliance QA, refusing to answer without authoritative support is correct
behaviour, so the system's abstention sentence is a meaningful output rather than
an error. This creates a structural hazard specific to multi-agent designs. If
the aggregator treats the presence of an abstention anywhere in the merged expert
output as an instruction to abstain, then a single abstaining expert vetoes every
other expert's contribution. Under that rule, every topology containing the
abstaining expert is silenced while topologies that route around it, or that run
no experts at all, are unaffected -- producing an apparent single-agent advantage
that is an artefact of the aggregation rule.

We therefore define the rule explicitly: the system abstains only when
\emph{every} expert that ran abstained. An expert that abstains is excluded from
aggregation and its abstention is recorded. We report two measures: the
\emph{system abstention rate}, the fraction of queries on which the final answer
is an abstention, and the \emph{expert abstention rate}, the mean fraction of
running experts that abstained. We regard this as a finding in its own right, and
recommend that any single-versus-multi comparison in a domain with an abstention
mechanism verify its aggregation rule before interpreting results.

\subsection{Fitting three experts on one consumer GPU}
\label{sec:memory}
Running three separate models rather than one shared model is what makes the
experts specialists, and it is also the design's binding practical constraint. We
report the memory budget in full because it determined the model selection, and
because the constraint that actually bit is not the obvious one.

The experiment GPU has 8188\,MiB of VRAM, of which 1120\,MiB was occupied by the
desktop environment, leaving 7068\,MiB. Weights are the smaller half of the
problem: the three 4-bit models occupy 5979\,MiB together
(Table~\ref{tab:experts}), leaving 1089\,MiB. The remainder must hold the
key/value caches of all three models while they generate concurrently, and KV
cache cost per token is governed by grouped-query attention (GQA), not by
parameter count. The three chosen models cost 112, 36 and 80\,KiB per token
respectively, or 912\,MiB in total at the concurrent-phase context length ---
inside the budget, with 177\,MiB spare.

Our first configuration failed on exactly this point, and the failure is
instructive. A 3.8B Phi-3.5-mini expert in the general-purpose slot was both the
largest in weights (2618\,MiB) and, because it has no GQA at all --- 32 key/value
heads against Llama 3.2 3B's 8 and Qwen2.5 3B's 2 --- cost 384\,KiB per token,
three to ten times its peers. All three models were resident with 252\,MiB
spare, so a naive weights-only check passed; their combined KV cache needed
roughly 2.1\,GB, so concurrent generation was impossible. Substituting Granite
3.1 2B, which has GQA, resolved it. \emph{The practical lesson for anyone
replicating a multi-expert deployment on a single accelerator is that attention
architecture constrains how many experts fit more tightly than parameter count
does, and that a residency check is not a sufficient feasibility test.}

Two further details matter for reproduction. The prompt-context ceiling
(6144 tokens) is set by the aggregator, which receives the retrieved context plus
up to three expert answers and so has the longest prompt in the graph at roughly
4500 tokens; the experts themselves need 1373--2141 tokens depending on
tokenizer. Because the aggregator runs alone, after the experts complete, this
ceiling does not multiply across three models --- budgeting three concurrent
models at the ceiling would reject a configuration that works. And we load the
library's own implementations of all three architectures rather than
repository-bundled modelling code, which is a correctness requirement rather than
a preference: the Phi-family bundled code uses a superseded KV-cache API and
fails on first generation under current \texttt{transformers}.

\subsection{Fine-tuning protocol}
\label{sec:finetuning}

\textbf{Datasets.} Each expert is trained on a corpus matched to its role:
LegalBench~\cite{guha2023legalbench} for the legal expert,
NewsQA~\cite{trischler2017newsqa} for the news expert, and
Dolly-15k~\cite{conover2023dolly} for the general-purpose expert. Dolly is
human-written, which avoids training on another model's outputs and the
provenance footnote that implies. We sample 1{,}500 examples per domain, yielding
1{,}425 training examples after deduplication and a 5\% held-out split --- enough
to induce a measurable specialisation effect without making training a multi-day
job --- and, because both the legal and news domains touch
AI-regulation-adjacent material, we remove cross-domain overlap: any news example
colliding with a legal example by normalised hash, or exceeding a token-Jaccard
threshold of $0.6$ against one, is dropped from the news set. Exact duplicates
within each domain are removed first. On this sample the cross-domain filter
removed nothing, which is the expected outcome for contract-law tasks against CNN
news articles and is reported rather than presented as a validated safeguard.

LegalBench is sampled round-robin across 102 of its 162 tasks rather than drawn
from one task, so the legal expert learns legal register broadly instead of
memorising a single answer format.

\textbf{Answer-format alignment, and why it was necessary.} LegalBench and NewsQA
are \emph{evaluation} benchmarks whose targets are short: measured over our
sample, 105 of the 112 loadable LegalBench tasks have mean answers of ten
characters or fewer, with a median of three (``Yes'', ``No'', ``UCC''), and NewsQA
answers are extractive spans averaging 26 characters. The deployed agents are
prompted for structured multi-sentence answers and scored against long reference
answers, so training directly on those targets teaches an expert to answer in
three characters and would depress judged completeness for reasons having nothing
to do with domain knowledge --- an artefact that would present as
``specialisation made quality worse''. We therefore render each target into the
response format the agent is served behind, using only content already present in
the dataset: for LegalBench, the task's question, the facts it supplies and its
ground-truth determination, arranged in the issue/application/conclusion skeleton
of the legal prompt; for NewsQA, the answer span together with the sentence of
the source article containing it. Fields the datasets do not provide --- notably
statutory citations --- are omitted rather than filled, since training a legal
assistant to produce plausible-looking citations it cannot support is the worst
available failure mode. No model generates any part of any training target. Mean
target length after this step is 438 characters for the legal set (from 3.8
before), 218 for news (from 26) and 368 for the general set, which needed no
transformation.

\textbf{Adapters and hyperparameters.} Each expert is trained with
QLoRA~\cite{dettmers2023qlora}: the base model is loaded in 4-bit NF4 with
double quantisation and a bfloat16 compute dtype, and remains frozen, while LoRA
adapters~\cite{hu2021lora} of rank $r=16$, $\alpha=32$ and dropout $0.05$ are
attached to the attention projections and the MLP projections. Training uses the
paged 8-bit AdamW optimiser, a cosine schedule with $3\%$ warmup, learning rate
$2\times 10^{-4}$, an effective batch size of 8 (batch 1 with 8 gradient
accumulation steps), a 512-token sequence limit and gradient checkpointing, for
3 epochs over 1{,}425 examples. Targets are rendered through each model's own
chat template, so an adapter is trained in the same format it is served in;
training on a different scaffold than deployment is a common and avoidable reason
a fine-tune appears not to have worked. Trainable parameters, final training
loss, held-out loss and wall clock per adapter are reported in
Table~\ref{tab:finetuning}. Hyperparameters were not tuned; they are standard
QLoRA defaults, and we report them as such rather than implying a search.

\textbf{An identical budget for all three experts.} The training budget above is
applied without variation across the three adapters, and this is a design
requirement rather than tidiness. If one expert received more epochs, longer
sequences or more examples than another, then any difference between topologies
would be partly attributable to which graph position happens to hold the
best-trained expert --- a second confound stacked on the model--position one
discussed in Threats to Validity, and one that would be entirely of our own
making. Consequently the sequence limit is 512 rather than the 1024 we first
used: at 1024 a single adapter took roughly four hours on the experiment GPU and
three would have taken eleven, and having established that, we retrained all
three from scratch at the reduced setting rather than let one adapter keep an
advantage the others could not be given affordably. Epochs were left at 3, as the
parameter most responsible for adapter quality and not the dominant cost.

Reducing the sequence limit is not free, and the way it fails is worth recording
because it is silent. Every one of our three datasets places its target at the
\emph{end} of the rendered example, so an example longer than the limit is
right-truncated into an input with no answer attached --- and a model trained on
such examples produces a flat, plausible-looking loss curve rather than an error.
Our first attempt at 512 tokens left 83\% of NewsQA examples over the limit
(NewsQA embeds a full article ahead of the question), and the resulting news
adapter did not learn: held-out loss moved from 1.920 to 1.956 across three
epochs with token accuracy static at 0.568, while the legal adapter over the same
budget improved from 0.919 to 0.844. The truncation rate was also badly uneven
across domains --- 83\% for news against 22\% for legal and 9\% for general ---
which is itself an inconsistency in effective training budget, of the same kind
the fixed hyperparameters are meant to exclude. We therefore cap each example's
input at 1400 characters and, for NewsQA, window the article around the answer
span rather than truncating from its start, bringing the over-limit rates to
0.6\%, 0.0\% and 5.0\% respectively; dataset preparation now measures rendered
token lengths with each model's own tokenizer and refuses to pass silently above a
10\% threshold. Serving is unaffected throughout: the sequence limit governs
training only, and serving prompts run 1373--2141 tokens.

Two honest qualifications to ``identical''. First, what is held identical is the
training \emph{budget} --- rank, scaling, epochs, learning rate, schedule,
sequence limit and example count --- not the resulting adapter capacity. Because
the three base architectures differ in their projection dimensions, the same rank
yields a different number of trainable parameters in each
(Table~\ref{tab:finetuning}), so the news expert receives modestly more adapter
capacity than the legal one. This is inherent to specialising three different
model families rather than one shared base, and it compounds the
model--position entanglement discussed in Threats to Validity rather than being a
separate problem. Second, held-out loss on the legal adapter reached its minimum
at epoch 2 and rose marginally at epoch 3 (0.844 to 0.848) while token accuracy
continued to improve, which is the onset of overfitting that the small training
set makes likely. We report it rather than silently selecting the best epoch: the
budget was fixed in advance across all three adapters, and re-selecting per
adapter would reintroduce exactly the inconsistency the fixed budget exists to
prevent.

\textbf{Adapter validation.} An adapter that leaves output essentially unchanged
would make the PEFT arm a relabelled copy of the control arm and RQ2 vacuous, so
each adapter was checked before benchmarking rather than assumed to have worked.
Two independent checks are applied. First, held-out cross-entropy against the
\emph{unadapted} base model: all three adapters reduce it substantially, by 46\%
to 72\% (Table~\ref{tab:finetuning}). Second, behavioural divergence, generating
the same domain probes from the adapted and unadapted weights and comparing them:
mean divergence was 87.2\% (legal), 80.6\% (news) and 87.1\% (general QA).

We report a measurement pitfall here because it produced a wrong conclusion
before it was caught. Evaluating once per epoch means the earliest measurement
already includes a full epoch of training, so an epoch-to-epoch comparison
answers only whether training \emph{continued} to improve. On that basis the news
adapter appeared inert --- its held-out loss ran 1.630, 1.638, 1.678 across three
epochs --- when in fact it had reduced loss from a base of 2.994 to 1.630 within
the first epoch and then saturated. Judging adapter quality from a training curve
without an unadapted reference point is therefore unreliable in exactly the
direction that would lead a practitioner to discard a working adapter, and we
measure against the base model instead.

\textbf{The general-purpose expert runs unadapted, and why.} Two of the three
experts carry their adapters in the PEFT arm; the general-purpose expert runs on
its base weights. This is a decision forced by measurement rather than a design
preference, and it is stated here because it changes what the PEFT arm is.

Its adapter is trained on Dolly-15k --- generic instructions with a median
rendered length of roughly 300 tokens --- while in this task the node is served
approximately 1370 tokens of statutory text, because it shares the retrieval
context given to every expert. At that length the adapted model emitted
ungrammatical output containing invented words and ran to the full 1024-token
generation limit on every call (156\,s), whereas the identical base weights
answered correctly in 9\,s, citing Annex III and the EU database registration
requirement. The legal and news adapters show no such behaviour; their training
examples resemble what they are served, at 299 and 642 median tokens
respectively. The general expert is also the smallest model and moved furthest
from its base in training (held-out loss 3.45 to 1.72), so it had the most
capability available to lose.

Three interventions were tried before accepting this. Biasing the Dolly sample
toward context-bearing examples raised the median from 175 to 301 tokens and
helped materially --- generation time fell from 213\,s to 78\,s and the model
stopped hitting the token limit --- but the output remained degenerate. Giving
Granite a padding token distinct from its end-of-sequence token, which it lacks,
had no measurable effect: the held-out curves before and after were
near-identical. Gentler hyperparameters were already in use. What remains is best
described as a distribution limit rather than a defect, and we report it as one:
\emph{parameter-efficient specialisation of a small model on general instruction
data degraded it on long out-of-domain context, to the point of unusability.}

The consequence for RQ2 is a narrower claim. The ablation compares two adapted
experts plus one unadapted against three unadapted, so it measures the effect of
specialising the legal and news roles rather than all three. We regard that as
the honest reading and prefer it to benchmarking a component we have measured as
broken, which would have made RQ1 a comparison of topologies over a degraded
expert.

\textbf{What the adapters learned, including what we did not intend.} All three
adapters shorten answers markedly relative to their base models: mean response
length on the validation probes fell from 161 to 68 words (legal), 68 to 24
(news) and 116 to 30 (general QA), and the legal adapter's rate of explicit
article citation did not increase. This is not a training failure --- the loss
reductions above are large --- but it is a transfer we did not intend. All three
source corpora have shorter targets than the deployed agents are prompted to
produce, and the adapters absorbed that answer register alongside the domain
content, notwithstanding the format alignment described above. Because judged
completeness and lexical overlap are both measured against long reference
answers, this biases the PEFT arm downward on precisely those metrics, and we
flag it here so the RQ2 result in Section~\ref{sec:results-ablation} is read with
the mechanism in view rather than as an unqualified verdict on specialisation.

\subsection{Dataset and reference answers}
The evaluation set contains 30 queries in three classes designed to test
RQ2: \emph{simple} queries answerable from a single statutory provision,
\emph{decomposable} queries requiring several sub-answers to be composed, and
\emph{routing} queries whose correct handling depends on selecting the right
domain. Reference answers were drafted against the statutory corpus and reviewed
by independent statutory alignment checks against official EU AI Act text. \emph{Reference answers
must not be described as expert-curated unless a domain expert has reviewed
them.} We report the model used to draft them so that judge self-preference can
be assessed.

\subsection{Metrics}
Metrics fall into five families.

\textbf{Reference alignment:} BLEU-1 and BLEU-4~\cite{papineni2002bleu};
ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2 and ROUGE-L F1~\cite{lin2004rouge}; word and character Jaccard;
TF cosine similarity; Levenshtein similarity.

\textbf{Judged quality:} accuracy, completeness and groundedness on a 1--5 scale
from an LLM judge~\cite{zheng2023mtbench}, plus their mean. Judge calls that fail
to return a parseable score are excluded rather than recorded as minimum scores.

\textbf{Retrieval:} precision@5, recall@5 and mean reciprocal rank against
human-annotated relevant chunks. These are computed only for queries whose
relevant chunks have been annotated by hand; the analysis pipeline suppresses them
otherwise. We note this explicitly because an earlier version of this dataset
recorded, as its relevance labels, the identifiers the retriever itself returned
while the reference answers were being drafted, which makes precision@5 equal to
$1.0$ by construction. Human relevance annotations were not performed for this dataset; retrieval metric columns (Precision@5, Recall@5, MRR) are therefore suppressed across all analyses.

\textbf{Operational:} end-to-end and backend latency, graph execution steps, mean
step latency, generation speed, measured prompt and completion tokens, cost
computed from measured tokens, and per-node latency.

\textbf{Behavioural and structural:} system and expert abstention rates, word,
sentence and paragraph counts, lexical diversity, citation counts.

Separating what a structure \emph{costs} from what its answer is \emph{worth} is
the crux of the single-versus-multi question, which is why both families are
reported for every configuration.

\subsection{Token-budget fairness}
A single agent that answers with fewer model invocations necessarily spends fewer
tokens, so an unadjusted comparison conflates two claims: that the single agent is
\emph{better} and that it is \emph{cheaper}. The recent single-versus-multi
literature controls for this by equalising the reasoning-token
budget~\cite{tran2026singleagent}. Because the topologies here differ in their
node structure rather than in a tunable budget, we cannot equalise tokens by
construction without changing the topologies themselves. We therefore report
quality both unadjusted and normalised by spend: judged quality per thousand total
tokens and per second of wall-clock time, and we include the normalised measure in
the significance tests. A topology that wins only on the unadjusted measure while
losing on the normalised one is buying its quality rather than earning it, and we
say so where that occurs.

\subsection{Statistical protocol}
The experimental unit is the query. Repeats within a (query, topology, arm) cell
are averaged before testing, because repeats are correlated samples of one item
and treating them as independent would inflate the sample size.

Two families of comparison are run, and Holm correction~\cite{holm1979simple} is
applied within each family separately, per metric.

\textbf{Topology (RQ1, RQ3).} All three pairs of topologies --- ALL vs.\
PARALLEL, ALL vs.\ DAG, PARALLEL vs.\ DAG --- are compared with a two-sided
Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\cite{wilcoxon1945individual} paired on query, with
Cliff's delta~\cite{cliff1993dominance} as a non-parametric effect size. These
tests are run \emph{within} an arm, never across one: pairing a tuned topology
against an untuned one would confound structure with specialisation. The same
tests are repeated within each task class.

\textbf{Specialisation (RQ2).} Within each topology, the PEFT arm is compared
against the base arm, paired on query. This is three tests, Holm-corrected across
that family.

Throughout, a positive median difference and a positive Cliff's delta both mean
the first-named side of a comparison scored higher. Comparisons with fewer than
six paired queries are reported as not tested rather than as null results, since a
two-sided signed-rank test cannot reach $p<0.05$ below that size. With 30 queries
the design reliably detects large effects only; non-significant results should be
read as inconclusive rather than as evidence of equivalence.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
Generation is fully local: three 4-bit NF4 quantised experts (Llama 3.2 3B
Instruct, Qwen2.5 3B Instruct, Granite 3.1 2B Instruct) served through
\texttt{transformers} with LoRA adapters via \texttt{peft}, co-resident on one
NVIDIA RTX 4070 Laptop GPU (8\,GB). Embeddings: nomic-embed-text via local
Ollama. Prompt-context ceiling 6,144 tokens with a 1,024-token generation budget;
prompts exceeding the ceiling are truncated from the left and the event is
recorded rather than silent. Decoding: non-deterministic (temperature sampling,
seeds 1000, 1001, 1002). Corpus: 735 chunks, comprising 644 chunks of EU AI Act
statutory text from 599{,}775 characters, ingested from CELEX:32024R1689 ---
Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 as published in the Official Journal --- retrieved
through the EU Publications Office's Cellar endpoint, plus 91 chunks from a
frozen snapshot of 32 news and commentary sources retrieved on 6 August 2026.
The news snapshot is fetched once and never refreshed during the run, so all 540
runs read an identical corpus; the ten routing queries ask for current
developments and are unanswerable from statute alone. Statutory chunks carry a
higher authority rank than news in the retriever's reranking, and the resulting
source mix per query type is reported in Section~\ref{sec:threats}. $N=30$ queries, 3 topologies, 2 arms, $R=3$ repeats
(540 evaluation runs). Judge: DeepSeek V4 Flash. Reference answers: initially
drafted by Llama 3.1 8B, then revised in full before scoring, with every
statutory citation checked against the ingested text of CELEX:32024R1689; 20 of
the 30 were rewritten against the statute alone and 10 against the statute plus
a frozen news snapshot (see Section~\ref{sec:threats}). Total execution time
across the 540 runs: $28.0$ hours ($18.5$\,h adapted, $9.5$\,h untuned).

Two caveats on the operational numbers. First, wall-clock comparisons between
parallel and sequential topologies are only meaningful to the extent that the
serving stack executes concurrent branches concurrently; we measure this directly
rather than assuming it, and report the result in Section~\ref{sec:concurrency}.
Second, all three experts share one GPU, so the parallel topologies contend for a
single compute resource --- a deployment on three accelerators would not be
subject to the same limit, and our latency findings for PARALLEL and DAG should
not be read as properties of those structures in general.

\subsection{Does nominal parallelism materialise?}
\label{sec:concurrency}

Before interpreting any latency number we establish whether the concurrent
topologies are concurrent in practice. With all three experts resident, issuing
three generation requests simultaneously from three threads completed in
$8.89$\,s against $9.52$\,s for the same three requests issued sequentially --- a
speedup of $1.07\times$, against a theoretical ceiling near $3\times$.

This is a hardware result, not a graph result: three models sharing one GPU
serialise at the level of compute regardless of how the orchestration layer
dispatches them. It has a direct consequence for the rest of this section.
PARALLEL and DAG pay the full coordination cost of a concurrent structure ---
extra model invocations, extra prompt tokens, an aggregation step over more
inputs --- while recovering almost none of the wall-clock benefit that motivates
concurrency in the first place. Their latency should therefore be read as close to
the sequential sum of their branches, and any latency advantage they show over ALL
comes from running fewer or shorter expert calls rather than from overlapping
them. On a multi-accelerator deployment this constraint would not apply, and we
are explicit that our latency findings for these two topologies are properties of
this deployment rather than of the structures in general.

\subsection{Operational profile (RQ3)}

\begin{figure*}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{fig1_operational.png}
\caption{Operational profile by topology (PEFT arm): end-to-end latency, latency
decomposed by graph node, execution steps, and cost from measured tokens. Error
bars are 95\% confidence intervals over queries and repeats.}
\label{fig:operational}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:operational} shows what an aggregate latency column cannot: the
node decomposition attributes each topology's cost to the stage that incurs it,
separating time spent generating from time spent coordinating.
End-to-end latency was $249.1$\,s for \textsc{all}, $244.2$\,s for
\textsc{parallel} and $246.8$\,s for \textsc{dag} --- a spread below $2\%$, with
no pairwise difference significant (Holm-corrected $p = 1.00$ throughout). In
\textsc{all}, whose branches execute in sequence, generation nodes (the three
experts plus the aggregator) accounted for $87.5\%$ of backend latency and
coordination for the remaining $12.5\%$. For \textsc{parallel} and \textsc{dag}
the per-node timings sum to more than the wall clock --- $314.2$\,s and
$223.9$\,s of expert time against backends of $243.9$\,s and $246.5$\,s --- both
because concurrent branches overlap and because each thread's timer additionally
absorbs the interval it spends blocked on a contended GPU. A generation
\emph{share} is therefore not well defined for those two topologies and we do
not report one. Coordination overhead was near-constant across all three at
$12.2$--$12.5\%$ of backend latency, which is the more informative comparison:
restructuring the graph moved work between branches without changing what
coordination cost. Execution cost from measured tokens was $\$0.00226$,
$\$0.00231$ and $\$0.00223$ per query for \textsc{all}, \textsc{parallel} and
\textsc{dag} respectively.

Because each expert is now a different model of a different size, per-node latency
is no longer comparable across experts in the way it was when all three shared one
model: a slower legal node may reflect Llama 3.2 3B's cost per token rather than
the legal task's difficulty. We therefore read the node decomposition as
attributing cost to \emph{positions in the graph}, not as a ranking of the
experts.

\subsection{Does coordination structure pay for itself? (RQ1)}
\label{sec:tradeoff-ref}

\begin{figure*}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{fig3_tradeoff.png}
\caption{Quality against latency (PEFT arm). The shaded region contains
topologies that are slower than the best-scoring topology without being better;
points in it are paying for coordination that returns nothing measurable.}
\label{fig:tradeoff}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff} converts the per-metric columns into a decision. With no
single-agent baseline, the reference point is the empirically best-scoring
topology rather than an external control, and the shaded region is the set of
configurations it dominates on both axes. That region is empty. \textsc{all}

significantly ($p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.92$ on quality, $1.00$ on latency). The
honest reading is not that one structure wins the trade-off but that, at this
scale, the trade-off does not discriminate: a practitioner choosing among these
three is choosing within measurement noise.

On token-normalised quality --- judged quality per thousand total tokens --- the
ranking was \textsc{all} $0.180$, \textsc{dag} $0.177$, \textsc{parallel}

expert invocations they make, so a structure could buy quality with tokens rather
than earn it with organisation; a topology that leads on the unadjusted measure
but not on the normalised one is doing exactly that.

no detectable quality effect in any topology, and in two of the three the point
estimate favours the \emph{untuned} arm. The same comparison on operational
metrics is emphatically not null. Specialisation increased end-to-end latency
in every topology with near-maximal effect sizes
($\delta = +0.92$, $+0.93$, $+0.98$ for \textsc{all}, \textsc{parallel} and
\textsc{dag}; $p_{\text{Holm}} \leq 8.3\times10^{-7}$) and raised token cost
significantly ($p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.004$, $0.0001$, $0.004$). A Cliff's delta
above $0.92$ means almost every adapted run was slower than almost every
untuned one. What specialisation did change in behaviour rather than score was
abstention: the expert abstention rate fell from $22.3\%$ untuned to $1.6\%$
adapted, driven by the legal expert, which declined to answer in $67\%$ of
untuned runs against $5\%$ adapted. System-level abstention was $0.000$ in both
arms, because the aggregator composed an answer from the remaining experts
whenever the legal expert withdrew --- so a change that is dramatic inside the
graph is invisible at its output.
Adapter validation before the run confirmed the adapters were not inert
(Section~\ref{sec:finetuning}), so a null result here would be a statement about
the value of this specialisation on this task, not about whether the adapters were
applied.

Two readings of this comparison are worth separating. If specialisation helps and
the topology ranking is the same in both arms, then coordination structure and
specialisation contribute independently, and the ranking in
Section~\ref{sec:tradeoff-ref} generalises beyond tuned experts. If the ranking
\emph{changes} between arms, then the best way to combine agents depends on
whether those agents are specialised --- which would be the more interesting
finding, and the one that most directly answers the question this paper set out
to ask. We observe that the nominal ranking is \emph{not} preserved: with
adapters the order is \textsc{all} $>$ \textsc{dag} $>$ \textsc{parallel}
($1.804$, $1.770$, $1.733$), while untuned it inverts almost exactly to
\textsc{dag} $>$ \textsc{parallel} $>$ \textsc{all} ($1.796$, $1.778$,
$1.752$). We decline to read this as the interesting finding above. No
within-arm difference is significant in either arm ($p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.92$
adapted, $1.00$ untuned), the largest gap in either ordering is $0.07$ judge
points against confidence intervals of roughly $\pm 0.10$, and a reordering of
three statistically indistinguishable values is what noise looks like. The
defensible statement is the weaker one: this experiment does not resolve
whether the best way to combine agents depends on their specialisation,
because it could not establish a ranking within either arm to begin with.

\subsection{Does decomposability change the answer?}

\begin{figure*}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{fig4_query_type.png}
\caption{Quality by topology within each task class (PEFT arm). If a coordination
structure earns its cost through decomposition, its advantage should widen from
simple to decomposable and routing queries.}
\label{fig:querytype}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:querytype} tests the standard defence of richer coordination
directly, and is the one result that cannot be read from a pooled table at all. If
sequential chaining or a converging dependency earns its overhead by decomposing
work, the gap between it and flat concurrency should widen on decomposable and
routing queries relative to simple ones. Within-class tests gave no significant
result in any class, but the direction is worth recording. On decomposable
queries --- the class the defence predicts --- \textsc{all} led both concurrent
structures by the widest margins observed anywhere in the study
(\textsc{all} vs \textsc{parallel}: median difference $+0.222$,
$p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.23$, $\delta = +0.19$; \textsc{all} vs \textsc{dag}:
$+0.111$, $p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.23$, $\delta = +0.23$), while \textsc{dag} and
\textsc{parallel} were indistinguishable from each other
($0.000$, $p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.67$, $\delta = +0.02$). On simple and routing
queries every comparison returned $p_{\text{Holm}} = 1.00$ with
$|\delta| \leq 0.14$. So the only place the sequential chain shows any
advantage is precisely where decomposition is supposed to pay --- but at
$p = 0.23$ with ten queries per class, this is a direction for a larger study
to test, not a result we claim.

%=============================================================================
\section{Discussion}
%=============================================================================

\emph{This section is written against the results above and cannot be finalised
until they are complete; the structure below fixes what each paragraph must
address.}

\textbf{Which structure wins, and why that is the shape it is.}
None of them wins, and that is the result. The three topologies are separated by
$0.07$ judge points against confidence intervals near $\pm 0.10$, with every
pairwise test returning $p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.92$ and $|\delta| \leq 0.07$; the
ordering even inverts between the adapted and untuned arms. We therefore cannot
adjudicate between the three mechanisms below, and we decline to narrate a
ranking that the statistics do not support. The one place any structure showed
an edge was the class where the decomposition argument predicts it: on
decomposable queries \textsc{all} led both concurrent structures by
$\delta = +0.19$ and $+0.23$, the largest effects anywhere in the study, which
is the direction that information flow between experts would produce --- but at
$p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.23$ over ten queries this is a hypothesis for a larger
study, not a finding. The mechanism matters
more than the ranking: a win for ALL would indicate that letting experts read
each other's output adds real information; a win for PARALLEL would indicate the
opposite, that hand-offs mostly add noise; a win for DAG would suggest the useful
quantity is one convergence point rather than either extreme.

\textbf{What specialisation bought.} It bought a large behavioural change and no
measurable quality gain, uniformly across topologies. Judged quality was
statistically indistinguishable between arms in all three
($p_{\text{Holm}} = 1.00$ throughout, $|\delta| \leq 0.08$), so the effect did
not differ by topology --- there is no interaction to report. What did change
was where the answer came from. Untuned, the legal expert declined on $67\%$ of
runs; adapted, on $5\%$. Yet system-level abstention was $0.000$ in both arms
and the final answers scored the same, because the aggregator simply composed
from the news and general experts whenever the legal expert withdrew. The
multi-agent structure absorbed the failure of a specialist, which is a useful
robustness property and simultaneously the reason specialisation could not be
detected at the output: the architecture had already covered the gap that
fine-tuning closes. Set against a near-doubling of latency
($\delta \geq 0.92$, $p_{\text{Holm}} \leq 8.3\times10^{-7}$) and significantly
higher token cost, the adapters bought a cleaner internal division of labour
that this system did not need. One asymmetry compounds this:
all three experts read the same retrieved statutory context, so a specialised
expert can only add value beyond what retrieval already supplies. That bounds how
much any amount of fine-tuning can contribute in a strongly retrieval-grounded
task, and is a reason to expect a smaller specialisation effect here than the same
adapters would show on a closed-book benchmark.

\textbf{Concurrency that is not concurrent.} The measured $1.07\times$ speedup for
three simultaneous experts is, in our view, the most transferable practical
finding in the paper. Topology work routinely treats parallel branches as free in
latency terms; on a single accelerator hosting multiple specialists --- the
configuration most likely in a privacy-constrained or on-premises deployment ---
they are not. A concurrent topology chosen for latency reasons should be validated
against the serving hardware before the choice is made, not after.

\textbf{Heterogeneous experts complicate attribution.} Because each expert is a
different model, a topology's result depends partly on which model sits in which
graph position. We can say which \emph{configuration} performed best; we cannot
fully separate that from which model happened to occupy the position the
configuration privileges. This is the principal interpretive cost of the
three-family design and we return to it in Threats to Validity.

\textbf{Practical guidance.} Four recommendations follow from these results
rather than from prior expectation. First, do not spend engineering effort
selecting among these coordination structures for a retrieval-grounded task of
this kind: they were indistinguishable on quality, on token-normalised quality
and on latency, so the simplest structure to build and debug is the rational
default. Second, validate concurrency against the serving hardware before
choosing a topology for latency reasons; three co-resident experts on one
accelerator returned $1.07\times$, and our concurrent topologies were no faster
end to end than the sequential chain. Third, measure specialisation at the
system output, not at the expert. Judged by its expert alone, fine-tuning here
looks transformative --- a $67\%$ abstention rate falling to $5\%$; judged at
the output it returned nothing measurable, because redundancy across experts had
already compensated. A team that instrumented only the specialist would have
concluded the opposite of what the end-to-end comparison shows. Fourth, and
following from that, treat architectural redundancy and component quality as
substitutes rather than independent goods: in a system with several experts and
an aggregator, investment in any one expert is bounded by how much the others
already supply, and that ceiling should be estimated before the fine-tuning
budget is committed.

%=============================================================================
\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}
%=============================================================================

\textbf{Model--position confounding: the principal limitation.} The three experts
are three different base models of different sizes and families, so any difference
between topologies is entangled with which model occupies which structural
position. DAG, for example, places the general-purpose expert (Granite 3.1 2B, the
smallest of the three) at its convergence point; if DAG underperforms, we cannot
cleanly separate ``a converging structure is worse'' from ``the smallest model is
a poor place to converge''. This is a direct and unavoidable consequence of the
three-family design, which was adopted deliberately so that ``specialist'' would
mean parameter-level differentiation rather than a prompt. A rotation design ---
permuting which model holds which role and re-running --- would resolve it and is
the single most valuable extension of this work. We state the limitation rather
than qualifying it away, and readers should treat the topology ranking as
applying to this assignment of models to roles.

\textbf{Construct validity.} Lexical overlap with a reference answer is a weak
proxy for legal correctness; a compliant answer phrased differently scores
poorly. We mitigate this with judged quality alongside lexical metrics, but the
judge is itself a language model subject to self-preference
bias~\cite{wataoka2024selfpreference}, which we mitigate by requiring the judge,
the models under test and the reference-drafting model to be distinct.
\emph{Unlike our earlier study, that requirement now holds: the judge (DeepSeek V4
Flash) generates none of the answers it scores, the three expert models are
open-weight models from three unrelated families, and the reference-drafting model
(Llama 3.1 8B) is distinct from all of them.} The self-preference caveat that
constrained the earlier work therefore no longer applies. Human validation of the
judge's agreement with expert assessment was not performed and remains a
limitation of the judged-quality metric itself.

A second construct concern is specific to the fine-tuning. Both LegalBench and
NewsQA are short-answer evaluation benchmarks, and we transformed their targets
into the response format the agents are served behind
(Section~\ref{sec:finetuning}). That transformation uses only real dataset
content, but it means the adapters are trained to produce a \emph{format} as well
as domain content, and part of any measured PEFT gain may be format conformance
rather than domain knowledge. Reporting both judged completeness and lexical
overlap partially separates these, but not fully.

\textbf{Internal validity.} Wall-clock latency depends on whether the serving
stack executes concurrent branches concurrently; rather than assume it, we measure
it and find it largely does not on a single GPU
(Section~\ref{sec:concurrency}). Abstention propagation, if unaddressed, biases
against topologies that run more experts; the all-abstain rule is stated
explicitly and its two rates reported separately. The two experimental arms are
served by separate processes, and the benchmark harness refuses to record runs
against a server whose loaded arm disagrees with the requested one, so the arms
cannot be silently mislabelled.

One consequence of that process separation is worth stating. Because the arms run
as consecutive passes rather than interleaved --- six co-resident models will not
fit, so the adapters cannot be swapped per query --- anything that varies slowly
over a multi-day run falls disproportionately on whichever arm ran second. On
consumer hardware the obvious candidate is thermal throttling: we observed a
same-class expert training at 7.15\,s per step against 5.5\,s earlier in the same
session at 81\,\textdegree C. Topology comparisons are insulated from this,
because topologies are interleaved across queries within an arm; the exposure is
specific to comparing \emph{latency} between arms. Each run therefore records its
absolute start time, and the analysis reports within-arm latency drift between
the first and last decile of each pass. Where that drift is large we report the
between-arm latency difference as uninterpretable rather than adjusting it away,
since a regression correction would conceal a measurement problem behind a
statistical one. Judged quality and reference-alignment metrics are unaffected.

\textbf{External validity.} A single corpus, a single domain, one assignment of
models to roles, and one 8\,GB single-GPU deployment. The concurrency finding in
particular is a property of that deployment. Model capacity may interact with
topology --- coordination plausibly matters more when individual agents are
weaker, and our experts are small --- so these results should not be extrapolated
to larger specialists without retesting.

\textbf{Statistical validity.} With 30 queries the paired tests detect large
effects reliably and small ones poorly; non-significant comparisons should be
read as inconclusive rather than as evidence of equivalence. The two comparison
families are Holm-corrected separately, which controls family-wise error within
each but not across both.

\textbf{Corpus provenance.} The statutory corpus was re-ingested from
CELEX:32024R1689 via the EU Publications Office's Cellar endpoint, because the
document URL used in our earlier work no longer serves content to
non-browser clients. The resulting corpus is the Official Journal text of the
Regulation and is therefore more authoritative than the pre-final adopted-text
version used previously, but it is not chunk-for-chunk identical, so retrieval
behaviour is not directly comparable between the two studies.

%=============================================================================
\section{Future Work}
%=============================================================================

Four extensions follow directly, in order of how much they would strengthen the
claims.

\textbf{Rotate models across roles.} The model--position confound identified above
is the most consequential limitation, and it is straightforwardly addressable:
permute which of the three models holds which expert role and re-run. With three
models and three roles this is six assignments, and it would separate the effect of
a coordination structure from the effect of which specialist sits at its critical
position. We regard this as the necessary next step rather than an optional
refinement.

\textbf{Test whether the concurrency ceiling changes the ranking.} Our measured
$1.07\times$ speedup means the concurrent topologies were evaluated under
conditions that deny them their main advantage. Repeating the comparison on
multiple accelerators, where three experts genuinely run at once, would show
whether the ranking is a property of the structures or of the hardware --- and
this is the experiment most likely to change the paper's conclusion.

\textbf{Vary specialisation strength.} We used one adapter configuration at one
training-set size. Sweeping LoRA rank and per-domain training volume would turn
RQ2 from a binary into a dose--response relationship, and would show whether
coordination structure matters more or less as agents become more sharply
specialised --- the natural continuation of the question this paper opens.

\textbf{Compare against learned topologies and a second domain.} Whether
learned communication graphs~\cite{zhang2024gdesigner, li2025argdesigner,
zhuge2024gptswarm} beat these canonical structures over parameter-differentiated
experts is open; so is whether any of this transfers beyond EU AI Act compliance
to a second retrieval-grounded domain, which is required before the conclusions
can be stated generally.

%=============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%=============================================================================

Existing evidence that multi-agent coordination fails to beat a single agent was
gathered under a condition that is easy to overlook: every agent ran the same
unmodified model, so the specialists being coordinated were one reasoner behind
several prompts. This paper asked what happens when that condition is removed. We
built a retrieval-grounded EU AI Act compliance system in which three domain
experts are three different open-weight models, each specialised by QLoRA on its
own domain corpus and all three co-resident on a single 8\,GB consumer GPU, and
compared three structurally distinct coordination topologies over them --- with
every topology also run over the identical untuned base models, so that
specialisation and structure could be separated rather than confounded.

No topology won: judged quality differed by at most $0.07$ points on a
five-point scale across \textsc{all} ($1.804$), \textsc{dag} ($1.770$) and
\textsc{parallel} ($1.733$), every pairwise comparison was non-significant after
Holm correction ($p = 0.92$, $|\delta| \leq 0.07$), and end-to-end latency
separated them by under $2\%$, so the coordination structure did not pay for
itself on any axis we measured. Nor did specialisation measurably improve
quality: the adapted and untuned arms were statistically indistinguishable
within every topology ($p_{\text{Holm}} = 1.00$, $|\delta| \leq 0.08$) while the
adapters nearly doubled latency ($\delta \geq 0.92$,
$p_{\text{Holm}} \leq 8.3\times10^{-7}$) and significantly raised token cost ---
though they did cut the expert abstention rate from $22.3\%$ to $1.6\%$, a
substantial change in the system's internal behaviour that its aggregator
rendered invisible at the output.

Two findings hold regardless of how those numbers land. First, on a single
accelerator, nominal concurrency is largely fictional: three co-resident experts
generating simultaneously ran only $1.07\times$ faster than the same work done
sequentially, so a topology chosen for its parallelism pays the full coordination
cost of that choice and recovers almost none of the benefit. Practitioners should
measure this on their own serving stack before letting it drive an architectural
decision. Second, the interpretive price of using genuinely different specialists
is that model identity and structural position become entangled; a rotation design
is required to pull them apart, and we identify that as the necessary next step
rather than claiming our ranking is already free of it.

We also note what this study deliberately does not claim. With three different
models in the graph there is no coherent single-agent condition, so nothing here
revises the single-versus-multi question --- it answers a different one, about how
best to combine specialists once you have them.
