Quick Answer:
The EU AI Act’s enforcement timeline was substantially rewritten on May 7, 2026, when the EU institutions reached a provisional agreement on the Digital Omnibus on AI. High-risk AI system obligations (Annex III) are now deferred to December 2, 2027 — 16 months later than originally scheduled.
August 2, 2026 — today — is the date Article 50 transparency obligations, GPAI penalty powers, and full national market surveillance authority activate.
Three new compliance deadlines also emerged from the deal.
EU AI Act Compliance Checker
⚡ The Situation on Day 0: What Changed, What Didn’t, What’s Next
The article you may have read in December 2025 described August 2, 2026 as the moment when “full enforcement activates” for high-risk AI systems. That was never accurate for the Annex III high-risk tier, and as of today it is a moot point: the Digital Omnibus superseded it months ago.
On May 7, 2026 — after a first trilogue collapsed on April 28 — the European Council, the European Parliament, and the European Commission reached provisional political agreement on the
Digital Omnibus on AI
. It is the first formal set of amendments to the EU AI Act since Regulation 2024/1689 entered into force on August 1, 2024.
The deal is not a retreat from AI regulation. It is a sequencing correction. Technical standards required for high-risk AI conformity assessment were not ready; penalizing companies for failing against non-existent benchmarks was legally incoherent. The institutions acknowledged that and moved the high-risk deadlines to dates when standards will actually exist.
What they did not do is delay everything.
Today is the date the Commission gains penalty enforcement powers over GPAI providers, the date Article 50 transparency obligations activate for most AI systems, and the date national market surveillance authorities can fully investigate and sanction AI Act breaches.

What August 2, 2026 Delivers, Today
Despite the high-risk delay, today is the most consequential single date in the Act’s remaining calendar. Three separate enforcement mechanisms activate simultaneously.
1. GPAI Penalty Powers Go Live
Providers of general-purpose AI models — including the large language models and foundation systems that now underpin enterprise AI stacks — have been legally subject to obligations since August 2, 2025: technical documentation, copyright compliance policies, training data summaries, downstream provider disclosures, and systemic risk assessments for models above 10^25 FLOPs.
The Commission could not fine anyone for violations during that first year.
As of today, it can.
The maximum penalty for GPAI non-compliance is €15 million or 3% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher. The AI Office — the Commission body with exclusive competence over GPAI models — has full investigatory authority from today: documentation requests, model access, on-site inspections, corrective measures.
For companies in the GPAI chain, the year of observation ends today.
2. Article 50 Transparency Obligations Activate
Article 50 applies to AI systems that interact with people, generate content, detect emotions, or categorize users biometrically. It is not limited to high-risk systems. The transparency obligations it creates cover the vast majority of deployed AI products, from the
AI-powered chatbots now deployed across industries
to generative content platforms serving EU audiences.
Chatbot and AI assistant disclosure:
Any AI system designed to interact with natural persons must inform those persons that they are interacting with an AI, unless the context makes it obvious. This applies at the first moment of interaction. The obligation falls on deployers.
Emotion and biometric categorization disclosure:
Systems that detect emotions or categorize users based on biometric data must notify users of this processing at the time of operation.
Synthetic content marking — new systems:
AI systems placed on the EU market from today onward that generate or manipulate synthetic audio, image, or video must embed machine-readable markers identifying outputs as artificially generated. The C2PA standard is the technical mechanism the Commission’s finalized Code of Practice on marking and labelling identifies by name.
Synthetic content marking — legacy systems:
Systems already on the market before today benefit from a grace period under the Digital Omnibus deal. Their watermarking obligation applies from December 2, 2026, not today. This four-month window (compressed from the originally proposed six months) is specifically for the machine-readable marking requirement, not for the other Article 50 obligations, which apply as of today.
Violations of Article 50 carry fines of up to €15 million or 3% of global annual turnover.
3. National Market Surveillance Authority Activation
Full investigatory and enforcement powers transfer to national market surveillance authorities in each member state today. These authorities gain the legal standing to investigate potential violations, demand documentation, order market withdrawals, and impose fines — not just for GPAI provisions, but for the full suite of obligations that have been building since February 2025.
Today is the date the “guidance phase” formally ends across the regulation — at least on paper. See “The Enforcement Gap,” below, for what that looks like in practice.
What Changed: The Digital Omnibus Deal in Full
High-Risk AI System Deadline Extension
The single largest change in the May 7, 2026 deal is a 16-month extension of the main high-risk AI enforcement deadline. To understand what this means for your organization’s AI in business strategy, it helps to know exactly which systems fall into which tier.
Annex III stand-alone high-risk systems
— which include AI used in employment decisions, credit scoring, biometric identification, critical infrastructure management, educational assessment, law enforcement, migration and border control, and the justice system — now face a compliance deadline of December 2, 2027. This replaces the original August 2, 2026 date.
Annex I product-embedded high-risk systems
— AI that serves as a safety component in regulated products including medical devices, machinery, lifts, radio equipment, and recreational watercraft — now face a deadline of August 2, 2028. This replaces the original August 2, 2027 date.
The rationale is technical, not political. CEN-CENELEC, the European standards bodies responsible for developing the harmonized standards that undergird high-risk AI conformity assessment, indicated those standards would not be available until Q4 2026 at the earliest. Requiring conformity assessment without the standards those assessments rely on would have produced inconsistent enforcement and created legal exposure for companies acting in good faith.
The delay does not cancel the obligations. Everything required for the original high-risk compliance date — risk management systems, data governance, technical documentation, quality management systems, conformity assessment, EU database registration, post-market monitoring, incident reporting, fundamental rights impact assessments — remains required. December 2027 is the enforcement date, not the starting point for preparation.
Machinery Regulation Moved from Annex I Section A to Section B
This structural change has practical significance for AI-enabled industrial equipment. The Machinery Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 has been reclassified from Annex I Section A (where full AI Act high-risk obligations applied in parallel with sectoral rules) to Section B (where sectoral rules are paramount). Under the new structure, the Commission must adopt delegated acts amending Annex III of the Machinery Regulation to incorporate AI-specific health and safety requirements by August 2, 2028. AI-enabled machinery is no longer caught in a dual-compliance model.
Refined “Safety Component” Definition
AI systems used solely for user assistance, performance optimization, efficiency, automation, or quality control will not qualify as “safety components” under Article 6(1) — and therefore will not be classified as high-risk under Annex I — unless a failure or malfunction of that system could actually endanger health or safety. This narrows the scope of Annex I high-risk classification in a way that will benefit many industrial and commercial AI deployments.
Two New Prohibitions: NCII and CSAM
The Omnibus adds two new prohibited AI practices to Article 5, effective December 2, 2026.
Non-consensual intimate imagery (NCII/NCIM):
AI systems that generate or manipulate realistic depictions of an identifiable person’s intimate parts, or that place identifiable persons in sexually explicit scenarios, without that person’s freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous, and explicit consent. This prohibition is colloquially understood to target “nudifier” applications — tools that generate synthetic intimate imagery from standard photographs.
Child sexual abuse material (CSAM):
AI systems that generate or manipulate CSAM, as defined in Directive 2011/93/EU, subject to a narrow carve-out where a “without right” defense applies under national law.
For providers, the prohibition extends beyond systems specifically designed for these purposes to any system where such generation is a reasonably foreseeable and reproducible outcome without significant technical modification — and where the provider has not implemented reasonable, proportionate, and effective safeguards to reliably prevent it. This is a design obligation, not just an intent-based restriction. Providers of general-purpose image and video generation systems must actively assess foreseeable misuse risks, even for models not marketed for intimate content.
The transitional period runs until December 2, 2026 — exactly four months from today. Companies with generative capabilities in scope have that window to either come into compliance or exit the EU market for those capabilities.
Expanded AI Office Supervisory Authority
The deal clarifies the AI Office’s exclusive competence in two domains:
First, AI systems built on GPAI models where the model and the system share the same provider or belong to the same corporate group are under the AI Office’s exclusive supervisory authority — not national market surveillance authorities. Certain carve-outs apply for Annex I products, critical infrastructure (Annex III point 2), law enforcement, border management, and financial institutions.
Second, AI systems integrated into Very Large Online Platforms (VLOPs) or Very Large Online Search Engines (VLOSEs) under the Digital Services Act are subject to AI Office enforcement ex post, with DSA risk assessment and audit obligations serving as the first point of entry for assessment.
For global AI developers serving EU markets through platform infrastructure, this centralizes their regulatory counterpart at the EU level rather than across 27 national authorities.
Sharpened Article 25 Information Obligations — Now With Fines
The information obligations an initial AI system provider owes to a downstream actor who becomes a provider of a high-risk system (for example, through substantial modification of the system) are now more specific. The initial provider must:
Make available technical documentation sufficient for the downstream provider’s compliance assessment
Disclose known limitations and failure modes
Provide targeted technical access, including for testing and validation
Critically, breaches of Article 25 now carry fines of up to 3% of worldwide annual turnover or €15 million — the same penalty band as other Tier 2 violations. This provision matters particularly in platform and API ecosystems where model providers supply foundation capabilities that downstream developers build into regulated applications.
AI Literacy: Language Softened
The amended text replaces “ensure AI literacy” with “take measures to support the development of AI literacy” among staff and persons dealing with AI systems on their behalf. The obligation to promote AI literacy among personnel remains; the obligation’s framing is now facilitative rather than absolute.
What Did Not Change
The Omnibus deal is narrowly scoped. The following remain exactly as written in the original AI Act:
All Article 5 prohibitions from February 2025
— social scoring, manipulative AI, real-time biometric identification (with law enforcement exceptions), untargeted facial image scraping, emotion recognition in workplaces and schools, criminal risk prediction based solely on profiling — all carry penalties of up to €35 million or 7% of global annual turnover and are fully enforceable now. The full text of these prohibitions is maintained in the
official EU AI Act regulatory framework
.
The four-tier risk classification architecture
— unacceptable, high, limited, minimal — is unchanged.
The GPAI compliance framework
(Articles 53–55) — unchanged. Documentation, copyright compliance, training data summaries, systemic risk assessment, adversarial testing, incident tracking — all still required, all enforceable as of today.
High-risk AI conformity assessment requirements
— unchanged in substance. The Omnibus only moved the deadline, not the obligations.
EU database registration for high-risk AI systems
— still required. The information that must be submitted has been simplified in narrow respects (Annex VIII, Section B, points 7 and 9 are deleted for systems self-assessed as non-high-risk), but the underlying registration requirement stands.
Penalty tiers
— unchanged: €35M/7% for prohibited practices, €15M/3% for high-risk and transparency violations, €7.5M/1.5% for misleading information to authorities.
GPAI Code of Practice: Current Status
The
General-Purpose AI Code of Practice
, published by the EU AI Office on July 10, 2025, remains the primary compliance pathway for GPAI model providers. As of today, the signatory base stands at 24 organizations: 23 full signatories — including Amazon, Anthropic, Google, IBM, Microsoft, Mistral AI, Aleph Alpha, and Cohere — plus xAI, which signed the Safety and Security chapter only.
Meta has not signed any chapter. xAI’s partial signature means it is committed to demonstrating compliance with transparency and copyright obligations via alternative adequate means — a position the Commission has indicated will face heightened scrutiny.
A Signatory Taskforce, chaired by the AI Office, has been established to facilitate coherent application of the Code across the signatory base.
The Code covers three chapters: Transparency (Article 53 documentation requirements), Copyright (training data and TDM opt-out compliance), and Safety and Security (for systemic risk models only, those trained above 10^25 FLOPs). Signing does not guarantee immunity from enforcement — the AI Office has stated it will not assume signatories to be acting in good faith without examination. However, Code commitments are a documented mitigating factor in fine calculation.
Update:
the separate Code of Practice on the marking and labelling of AI-generated content — relevant to Article 50(2) obligations — was still in second draft when this article was last substantively written, with finalization “expected by mid-2026.” It has since been finalized. By July 31, 2026, roughly 190 organizations had signed it: 83 on the provider-facing Section 1 (including Google, Microsoft, OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, and Mistral) and 152 on the deployer-facing Section 2 (including Getty Images, Lufthansa, and Iberdrola). Notably, several companies that declined the GPAI Code — Meta among them — signed this one; the two codes cover different obligations and attract different signatories. Who Must Act as Enforcement Begins Today
The 16-month extension for high-risk AI systems has created a misimpression that the August deadline is now irrelevant. It is not. The following categories of organization have live obligations as of today.
Any organization providing a GPAI model:
Penalty enforcement begins today. If you have not implemented the GPAI Code of Practice or established an alternative adequate compliance demonstration, your exposure is active.
Any organization deploying a chatbot or AI assistant in the EU:
The Article 50 chatbot disclosure requirement — informing users they are interacting with an AI — applies today. This is a deployer obligation, not a provider obligation. If you use a third-party model, you still own the disclosure.
Any organization deploying emotion recognition or biometric categorization AI:
The Article 50 notification requirement for these systems applies today.
Any organization whose AI systems generate or manipulate synthetic content (new systems only):
Watermarking obligation applies today for systems first placed on the EU market on or after this date.
Any organization already in violation of February 2025 prohibited practices:
National market surveillance authorities gain full investigatory authority today. The window for self-correction before active enforcement narrows significantly — though see “The Enforcement Gap,” below, on how unevenly that authority is actually staffed.
Any organization providing downstream AI applications built on GPAI models:
If you substantially modified a GPAI model or build systems classified as high-risk, the Article 25 obligations — and their associated 3% fine tier — now apply to your supply chain relationships. For context on how widely these models are now integrated into business operations, see our
AI statistics analysis
.
What December 2, 2026 Requires
Three hard obligations converge on this date, now 122 days away.
Watermarking for legacy generative AI systems:
Systems placed on the EU market before today must embed machine-readable markers in synthetic audio, image, and video outputs by December 2. The C2PA content credential standard is the leading technical implementation. Adobe Firefly, OpenAI DALL-E 3, OpenAI Sora, and Google Imagen already deploy C2PA in production. Midjourney is the most prominent generative tool that does not, and it faces direct exposure unless its approach changes before this date.
NCII/CSAM prohibition:
AI systems that generate non-consensual intimate imagery or CSAM — and systems that lack reasonable safeguards against being used for these purposes — cannot be placed on the EU market or used in the EU from December 2, 2026. For providers of general-purpose image and video generation tools, this is an active design obligation requiring foreseeable misuse assessment.
National regulatory sandbox establishment:
Member states were originally required to establish AI regulatory sandboxes by today. This deadline was extended to August 2, 2027 under the Omnibus deal, giving national authorities additional time to operationalize testing environments for innovative AI.
What December 2, 2027 Requires: The New High-Risk Horizon
For most compliance teams, December 2027 is now the primary planning horizon for high-risk AI systems. The obligations due by that date are unchanged from what was originally required by August 2026; only the deadline has moved. A brief summary for planning purposes:
Providers of Annex III high-risk systems must have operational risk management systems, representative and quality-controlled training data, comprehensive technical documentation, automatic logging and record-keeping, deployer transparency documentation (instructions for use), human oversight mechanisms appropriate to the risk profile, and demonstrated accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity performance. They must complete conformity assessment — internal for most Annex III systems, third-party for biometric identification and certain others — obtain CE marking authorization, and register in the EU database before market placement.
Deployers of Annex III high-risk systems must assign qualified personnel to oversight, ensure input data quality, maintain automatic logs, report serious incidents to providers and authorities, inform affected persons of AI use, and conduct fundamental rights impact assessments where required under Article 27.
One structural implication of the extension: conformity assessment queues that were building toward August 2026 have now partially dispersed. Organizations that began engagement with Notified Bodies in early 2026 may find scheduling more manageable — but those beginning in 2027 may find the same queues reconstituted ahead of the December 2027 deadline. The sensible approach is to continue preparation at pace rather than treating the delay as additional planning time.
GPAI Systemic Risk: What the Largest Models Still Face
Models trained using more than 10^25 floating-point operations — designated by the Commission as GPAI models with systemic risk — carry enhanced obligations under Article 55 that have been applicable since August 2, 2025, with full penalty enforcement beginning today. These include:
Model evaluation identifying and mitigating systemic risks, including adversarial attacks, cybersecurity vulnerabilities, risk amplification through model cascades, and major accident scenarios. Adversarial testing through red-teaming exercises simulating malicious use. Comprehensive incident tracking, documentation, and reporting to the AI Office. Adequate cybersecurity protection for the model and its physical infrastructure.
The Future of Life Institute estimates 5 to 15 companies worldwide currently sit above this threshold. The models currently in or near this category include GPT-4 and successors, Claude Opus models, advanced Gemini versions, and frontier models approaching human-level cognitive capabilities across broad task domains. The Enforcement Gap: What’s Happening in Member States
Legal authority activates across the EU today. Staffing does not automatically follow. According to the Future of Life Institute’s national implementation tracker, as of June 17, 2026 — the most recent published figures — only
9 of the EU’s 27 member states
had fully designated both a market surveillance authority and a notifying authority. Twelve had partial designations (draft legislation, an announcement, or one authority appointed), and six had designated neither.
That gap does not exempt anyone from the law. The AI Act applies to providers and deployers regardless of national enforcement readiness, and the AI Office’s authority over GPAI models is centralized and does not depend on member state staffing at all. But it does mean enforcement will be uneven for a while: a company operating in Ireland (15 designated authorities, among the most built-out) and a company operating in Austria (no authority designated) are subject to the identical statute today, with very different odds of a national regulator actually knocking on the door in the near term.

Global Regulatory Convergence Update
The EU AI Act’s influence on global regulatory frameworks has accelerated in 2026:
Colorado AI Act
(effective February 2026) requires developers of high-risk AI systems in consequential decision contexts to use reasonable care to avoid algorithmic discrimination — closely mirroring the AI Act’s approach without the comprehensive documentation and conformity assessment requirements.
Brazil
passed its AI bill in September 2025 (awaiting presidential signature), establishing a risk-based classification system with explicit reference to the EU framework and penalties for non-compliance.
Canada’s AIDA
within Bill C-27 continues its parliamentary process, proposing high-impact system designations and ministerial audit authority, with penalties up to CAD $25 million or 5% of global revenue.
UK
maintains its principles-based sectoral approach but faces increasing pressure as UK companies serving EU customers discover they must achieve AI Act compliance regardless of lighter home-country rules.
The “Brussels Effect” that GDPR demonstrated — where the EU regulatory framework became the de facto global standard due to market access requirements — appears to be repeating for the AI Act. Organizations building single global
AI governance
programs are increasingly anchoring them to the EU framework as the common baseline.

Frequently Asked Questions
Did the EU just cancel the August 2026 AI Act deadline?
No. Today remains a significant enforcement date. What changed on May 7, 2026 is that the high-risk AI system obligations under Annex III have been deferred to December 2, 2027. The GPAI model penalty powers, Article 50 transparency obligations, and national market surveillance authority all activate today, as originally scheduled.
Does the Digital Omnibus delay mean I can stop AI Act compliance work?
No. The Omnibus deals with the enforcement timeline, not the obligations themselves. Everything required for high-risk AI compliance must still be completed — the deadline for enforcement has shifted, not the work. For GPAI and Article 50 obligations, today’s date was never in question.
What is the GPAI Code of Practice and do I have to sign it?
It is a voluntary compliance framework published by the EU AI Office on July 10, 2025, covering three chapters: Transparency, Copyright, and Safety and Security. Signing is not legally mandatory, but it provides a strong presumption of compliance and the Commission treats Code commitments as a mitigating factor in fine calculation. As of today, 24 organizations have signed at least one chapter. Non-signatories who demonstrate equivalent rigor through alternative means may also satisfy their obligations, but they do so without the procedural benefits Code membership provides.
What AI systems does Article 50 cover?
Article 50 applies to any AI system that interacts with natural persons, generates or manipulates synthetic content (audio, image, video, text), detects emotions, or categorizes users biometrically. It is not limited to high-risk systems. Most deployed AI products — chatbots, content generators, voice assistants, recommendation engines, virtual agents — fall within at least one Article 50 obligation.
When does high-risk AI system compliance become mandatory under the new timeline?
Under the May 2026 Digital Omnibus deal, stand-alone Annex III high-risk AI systems (employment, credit, biometrics, law enforcement, education, migration, justice, critical infrastructure) must comply from December 2, 2027. High-risk AI embedded in regulated products under Annex I must comply from August 2, 2028.
What is the new NCII/CSAM prohibition and when does it apply?
The Digital Omnibus added a new Article 5 prohibition on AI systems that generate or manipulate non-consensual intimate imagery (including so-called “nudifiers”) and child sexual abuse material. The prohibition applies from December 2, 2026. For providers, it covers not just systems designed for these purposes but any system where such generation is a reasonably foreseeable and reproducible outcome without adequate safeguards.
My company deployed an AI chatbot in the EU in early 2026. Do I need to add an AI disclosure?
Yes. Article 50(1) requires that users be informed they are interacting with an AI system at the moment of first interaction, unless the artificial nature is obvious from context. This obligation activates today for deployers. If your chatbot does not currently surface such a disclosure, it needs to as of now.
What is watermarking under Article 50(2) and what standard should I use?
Article 50(2) requires that AI systems generating or manipulating synthetic audio, images, or video embed machine-readable markers in their outputs that identify the content as artificially generated. The Commission’s finalized Code of Practice on AI-generated content marks C2PA (Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity) as the reference standard; roughly 190 organizations had signed the code by July 31, 2026. For systems already on the EU market before today, the December 2, 2026 grace period applies for this specific requirement.
What enforcement actions have actually been taken so far?
No public penalties have been issued as of today. The Commission launched the first formal investigations into potential prohibited AI practices in early 2026. Several national data protection authorities have signaled intent to coordinate with AI Act enforcement on matters involving biometric data. Enforcement has been in an infrastructure-building phase; today’s activation of penalty powers is expected to shift the regulatory posture from observation to active enforcement over the coming months. The GDPR experience — slow start, then landmark fines — is the appropriate precedent to calibrate expectations.
How does the EU AI Act interact with GDPR?
The AI Act and GDPR operate concurrently for AI systems that process personal data. Data protection authorities retain full jurisdiction over personal data processing aspects alongside AI Act market surveillance authorities. Many AI Act obligations — data governance under Article 10, fundamental rights impact assessments under Article 27, logging and transparency — overlap in practical scope with GDPR data protection impact assessments and data minimization requirements. Organizations should integrate their AI Act and GDPR compliance programs rather than treating them as parallel workstreams.
Does the Omnibus affect GPAI models already on the market before August 2025?
No change here. GPAI models placed on the market before August 2, 2025 have until August 2, 2027 to achieve full compliance. This transition period was established in the original AI Act and was not modified by the Omnibus.
